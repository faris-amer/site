# man, 
Men aren't doing so hot right now. Men on average have higher self-percieved mental health, but consist of 75% of the suicides in the west, commit more violent crime, are more likely to live in extreme isolation, are more likely to be alcoholics or abuse other drugs, etc, etc.

Recently, a major trend of men becoming more right-wing and women becoming more left-wing, along with the radicalization of both groups has led to less relationships overall between men and women - incel culture is skyrocketing, and it seems like most men online have gotten a little insane. While scrolling, have you ever come across a video of a woman who's playing a sport, lifting, painting, singing, is black, is white, is asian, or (and this is the one I'm most ashamed of) is Muslim? The comments section would have you think she'd killed a man on camera with the amount of vitriol that's spewed. 

But I'm not just here to call men evil, I am a man after all, and want the best for my people. 

Before I get into what I wanna say, some exposition:

Men and women are different in significant ways, heavily influenced by society and the people around us. If biologically we're 70% similar and 30% different, societally we're 10% similar and 90% different. That's slowly not becoming the case, and it's causing a few things to happen. Let's start with the ideal man/woman as portrayed in media and stories and society at large; this idea of the male vs female power fantasy. These power fantasies are seen everywhere, from our role models to our celebrities and especially in media like movies and TV shows. The male power fantasy is self-evident: Be strong, good looking, charismatic, rich, funny, powerful, and most importantly *respected by your peers*. Movie franchises like *Indiana Jones*, *Mission Impossible*, the *Rocky* movies, *The Karate Kid*, every superhero movie released before this cultural shift, and so much more, even today. Now this isn't terrible at all, in fact it's pretty awesome - being rich and powerful and respected by the people around you, and always getting the girl and beating the bad guy at the end of the movie, it instills this idea that if you just try hard enough at anything, you will succeed. As long as you're strong and smart and can fight anything that comes your way, you will succeed in life. And this worked, for a time. As for the female power fantasy: well... there really isn't one. The closest thing to a 'power fantasy' is getting to be the most beautiful and bestest looking girl in the world so that prince charming will pick you up at the ball. 

For example, let's take 2 very similar Disney movies: Cinderella and Aladdin. Both follow a destitute main character who's ostracized by society (Cinderella by her sisters, Aladdin by the guards) until a magical being comes into their lives, granting them their biggest wish. Cinderella transforms into a beautiful princess, Aladdin into a royal prince, and they both go to find their match. In both stories, the monarch of the area they're in is trying to find a match for their prince charming/princess jasmine. Cinderella captivates the prince with her beauty, proving her evil and ugly stepfamily wrong, and prince charming searches the lands for her, and then chooses her over her siblings, proving that she really is the prettiest one of them all. Aladdin captivates princess jasmine by taking her on an adventure, showing her the world - she's not materialistic and instead falls in love with his humor, his personality, how different he is from the rest of the princes she's seen. Eventually, Jafar makes an appearance, and then Aladdin bests him, once again beating the bad guy and claiming the girl for his own. 

These tales have been told in thousands of variations for centuries. Cinderella is based on a French folk tale from the 1600s, and the origins of that tale trace back to ancient Greek and Chinese folklore. Aladdin on the other hand stems from the Arabic 1001 Nights (Also known as "Arabian Nights") which comes from the ninth century, and was later adopted by Europeans in the 18th century. Point being, these aren't inventions of an evil corporation or awful western values, this is just How Things Were for a long, long time. And this was reflected in real life: Men worked and provided material support, women were homemakers and provided emotional support. But things change. Women got tired of their greatest aspirations in life being just the most attractive to a man, and wanted to share in the "male power fantasy". Women's suffrage happened (insert joke here), and the first and second-wave feminist movements of the 20th century saw women voting, in universities, in the workplace, in governance, and in more leadership positions than before. Cut to today, and now more women are entering post-secondary education than men are, and academically dominate both in performance and population fields like psychology, biology, social sciences, and now a majority of working professionals with at least a bachelor's degree are women. But we're here to talk about men, right?

## it's just a feeling

There's an aspect to life many men are unaware of, that I was ignorant of as well - the idea of emotional awareness, being able to see how your words affect others, how others' words affect you, and the various complexities of the social ecosphere around you. Previously, as a man all you had to do was be able to keep a stable job, and look decent, and you'd be able to land a relationship because that's what women needed. You could get away with being a little arrogant or immature because you could make up for it with your social status, your wealth, etc. Today, it really isn't enough to just be a breadwinner. More on this idea of emotional awareness - men and women's relationships look a lot different.

Male friendships, generally, are deeply impersonal. Guys can be best friends, brothers, see each other every day, would die for each other, and yet, still know almost nothing about one another. 

There's a column in The Atlantic called 'Dear James' where readers ask James questions. [One reader asks](https://www.theatlantic.com/culture/2025/10/dear-james-male-friends-nongrowth-mindset/684552/?utm_source=facebook&utm_medium=paid&utm_campaign=2025_Content_FullPlacements_Prospecting_Sales_Standard&utm_content=102025_DearJamesGuyFriends_NA_NA_LearnMore&utm_term=FullPlacement_Advantage&referral=FB_PAID&utm_id=6754888483877&fbclid=PAZXh0bgNhZW0BMABhZGlkAAAGM9cvYT1zcnRjBmFwcF9pZA81NjcwNjczNDMzNTI0MjcAAadPRcwqwZYTkaOsCxCDRSOOGDOp9hBhVBcQpe-gZy268kzTj6Uy2BbhCMO_pQ_aem_5_guDF-xGWJv4krb1E7oOA&campaign_id=6754888483877&ad_id=6819593294477):

*Dear James,*

*Every Thursday for the past decade, I’ve sat with the same group of guys for a beer after work. I don’t think any of them has changed a bit in 10 years. Nothing. They’ve done nothing to grow themselves or their talents. Each one of them, if they were to die today, would get nearly the same eulogy: Nice man. Worked hard. Loved his children. Nothing wrong with any of that. Or is there?*

*I love them—and also admit that I’m judging them. I can’t help wondering if they feel any compulsion to better themselves, to help their neighbors, to serve others. What do you think is our obligation to think of people beyond ourselves?*

James responds:

*Dear Reader,*

*Here’s a question for you.*

*Do you really love these guys? Do you appreciate each one in his radiant singularity, while knowing in your heart that behind and beyond this singularity you share the same immortal, compassionate essence? Can you look at any one of your buddies holding a beer and getting louder (or quieter) as the evening progresses, and recognize his struggle as a child of God in a fallen world?*

*Probably not, right? Because if you loved them like that, you’d know that plenty has happened in their lives in 10 years, and that they have most certainly changed or been changed.*
*...*

The reader can't possibly fathom anyone around him grown, changed, experienced things, even after over a decade of knowing these guys. Why is it this way? Are they ignorant, or have they just never been shown anything to the contrary?

The game is simple, friends go around in a circle and ask each other questions about themselves. It's amazing just how little these people knew about each other, and it made me think about how little I knew about my friends. Some of my favorites (all of which couldn't be answered by any of the friends):

*How old am I?*

It goes on and on, and gets progressively more depressing behind the airs of drinks and a funny video idea with the boys. Think to yourself, how much do you *really* know about your friends? and how much do they know about you? Why don't we share these things about ourselves as men? It's nothing about our biology or male lizard brains, it's all about the ways we react. Throughout the video and its sequel, some real personal topics were brought up, and the group's first instinct was to laugh at every single one, then the gravity kinda hits and they all turn serious. We almost never uplift our friends, and in turn, our friends almost never uplift us.

In *Superbad*, lifelong friends Seth and Evan have trouble dealing with the fact that they're moving to different colleges after high school. They've been inseparable, but refuse to acknowledge the fact that they won't see each other again. Seth is insecure about his academic performance and sees Evan's choice to go to Dartmouth as an insult to their friendship, and Evan can't muster up the courage to tell him his plans, because he knows it'll hurt Seth's feelings. They spend the entire movie putting each other down, belittling each other at every chance, because they can't fathom another option.

Think about this - if you were to take a personal risk, step out of line, how many of your friends would support you? How many in your friend circle would support another friend if he were to start a business, or a social media page, or start doing standup comedy? Could you watch your friend bomb on stage and be the only person laughing in the room? How many times have you seen the dumbest person in your friend group genuinely try to study, but it somehow only provides more joke fodder? We have trouble showing genuine care for our friends, and we don't know how to recieve (or ask for) care from our friends. Jokes are funny, it's really, really fun to roast and ragebait your friends into arguing, etc. But balance in everything is key.

Contrast this to the movie *Mean Girls*, where the main character is a homeschooled kid from another country, with almost no social intuition for the complex web of the american high school. Immediately, she's taken in by (multiple) groups, and educated on the norms, things to do and not to do, told when she's looking good or bad or friendly or mean and all this reassurance and social training pays off almost immediately.

When's the last time you've been with a friend who *really stinks* and took time to inform them, or help them get a fresh change, wherever you were? When's the last time your friend said something really rude, or unfunny, or went out in a bad looking outfit, etc, etc, and you pulled them aside and warned them? You might not be the most charismatic, best dressed, or best looking, but some things are a bit obvious from an outside point of view. These small considerations build up, and build an environment where you genuinely care about your friends and they start to care about you. There's a reason women are a lot more okay not being in relationships than men are, and this is exactly why - even without a partner, they've got their girlfriends to fall back on.

## falling down, and up again

If you're working somewhere where you need to be physically present, and suddenly lose your car, or break a leg, or are hospitalized for any reason and unable to work for a few months at a time - the effects could be devastating. If you live alone, are renting an apartment, and don't have much savings, in a month or two you're in deep debt, handicapped and unable to work, and evicted from your apartment because you can't pay rent. But if you have family that lives a few hours away, it'll still suck, but at least you have a place to live and heal up, and while after you're healed you've still got to go and find a new job and apartment, you're nowhere near as bad as the guy who's been crashing on random people's couches while racking up credit card debt to survive while ill.

It's just an example, but the same applies to every field in life - especially your social life. If you have strong relationships with people you can talk to about real things, you can bounce back much, much faster than someone who doesn't. There's a reason why in most cultures, when someone's close family member passes away, people come from far and wide, no matter how familiar or unfamiliar they are with the deceased and their family, to give their condolences and comfort the grieving. Losing someone like that can be taxing on you mentally, and physically - much worse than a broken leg. We aren't invincible, and as much as we'd like to think it, we can't just retreat into our fortress of solitude until we're over it. 

But this applies to much smaller things as well - a rejection from a job, a rejection from a girl, a fight with a close friend, family drama, and all the other wonderful feelings life has to offer. 

On the first day of engineering at Western, the Dean took us all into a lecture hall and gave us a talk. It wasn't about hard work, or trying our hardest, or being really ethical in our work, he had one very clear point to make: *Engineering is a team sport*. "If you think you can go to your classes, go home, study hard, and do well, you're dead wrong" were the words that came out of his mouth, and every moment of the next 4 years only reinforced this sentiment. <br/> The university put us in excellent circumstances to realize this ethos. The entire first-year class had the exact same courses, and was split into two groups. Each group had the same lecture times, and there was a lot of overlap in our labs and tutorials; assignments were often take-home or done in groups to encourage this. Even if you had no social skills, or knew nobody before coming in, you'd see the same people so often all day that it'd be almost hard not to make a few friends along the way, if only to have someone to sit beside during the lectures who knew your name. The classes were tough, and there was so much content that you could never really be 100% on top of every assignment, every due date, notes on every lecture - eventually you'd be sick or miss a class or not know how to find something, and you'd reach out for help. And people are - in my experience - more than happy to help. <br/> Why is this? Because a) they were *told* that engineering was a team sport - we weren't competing against each other, we were all struggling to stay afloat together. And b) we were all helped in a major way at the beginning of the year, by the upper-years. If you know, you know. 

An initial act of generosity by your peer leaders and a clear ethos set by the authority immediately turned 900 students into a collective body. Ever behind on an assignment? You can bet someone knows someone who's got the answer sheet for the past 5 years of assignments, and is ready to share it at no charge. Can't understand some question the professor took up? Someone smarter than you took the most beautifully annotated notes and is willing to explain it to you. And you best believe, when that one guy who's shown up to maybe 3 lectures all year but you saw once in the hallway and is chill with your friend asks you for the answers to Assignment B, you're going to give it to him, because that's just how things work here. Any attempt at monetizing resources or gatekeeping are frowned upon, and snitching is basically a death sentence.

Why does this come so naturally to us, and yet emotionally we're so inept? It's because of the environment you're raised in. Now this doesn't apply to everyone - chances are, if you're from a well-off family, have a few siblings, a few family friends, extended family around you, attended events where you need to act a certain way, say certain things, were able to travel the world and see different cultures, you've already got a head start compared to everyone around you.

## waking up

![](/media/pictures/badpartner.png)

When I was 6 years old, I got on a bike for the first time. I had no idea what a bike was before then, short of pictures in spelling books. I didn't understand what the handles did, where to put my feet, how to pedal. Once I understood what a bike was, I understood how it could help me get places, and how I had to improve my balance, speed, and endurance if I wanted to be any good at riding the bike. The same applies to the emotional world around you. The first barrier to overcome is to realize that it exists, there exists this thing where you feel things, and the people around you feel things, and you need to pay attention to this. The words you say, the questions you ask, and the jokes you make have real impact on those around you. Typically the first instance this happens is when you hurt someone around you out of ignorance - but it doesn't just take that. Plenty of people go around hurting the people they love with no clue why, and since people typically get defensive and/or leave, they're left without answers. It takes someone who *really* cares about you, who's willing to forgive you, and explain to you why your actions were hurtful, and it takes a real, heartfelt apology from you to repair that bond. Since we're never explicitly taught this, the only way to really learn is to make mistakes, which is not a good course of action. Making mistakes when cooking pasta is fine, making mistakes with people you care about isn't.

After you realize your shortcomings, then you need to put in the work to improve upon it. If people say you're emotionless, or you hear things like "man I totally thought you hated me when we first met", or you realize that you're getting a lot more birthday gifts than you're giving out, it's time to make a change. 

This 'switch' moment is very memorable, and very clear - and I feel like anyone who's been able to see the world and maintain relationships with their close friends can pinpoint a single event or series of events that opened their eyes to this. 

![](/media/pictures/balding1.png)

There's a reason male role models are so influential on guys, way more than their peers. Ask any guy and they can point out a father, a coach, a teacher, an older brother, an upperclassman that they look up to and respect. And if you're lucky, they're good men with good lessons to teach, valuable information to pass on. There's a severe lack of male role models today, and while the stereotype of fatherless women exists, fatherless men end up much worse.

Teachers are overwhelmingly female, there are much more single mothers than single fathers, much more fathers who work abroad or work off-hours and can't spend time with their kids, and that lack of role model leaves a young man more or less aimless. Without having inherited any lessons, maturity, discipline, or respect from the people in your life, you wander aimlessly with your ego, insecurities, and physical capability like a ticking time bomb for everyone around you. You can't connect with any of your friends, which builds loneliness - you can have all the friends in the world, but if you never truly connect, if you don't have that bond between real, close friends, you feel alone against the world, and don't have any safety net to fall back on. If you were walking along a cliff and had no net below to catch you, what would you feel? Only one emotion is rational here: fear.

You start to blame the world for your problems, and there's no shortage of people around you who are ready to profit off your fear. Is it the evil billionaires who want to keep you in the rat race? Is love dead, and it's the evil women who's standards are too high? Is it the Rothschilds personally plotting against you? Is that really the reason you're like this? These paths all lead to one destination - it's not good. 

Previously, you needed friends purely for the reason to have something to do - the internet's been around for a while, but never has it had such a stranglehold on our attention spans. You can meet "people" online and play games and watch shows and stay infinitely entertained, so there's no reason to go outside, have a walk, have a kind of awkward conversation with the one guy who's free that day when you could be inside having so much more fun. This however doesn't create any new life experience, doesn't grow you as a person, and as your mental and physical health deteriorate, you become much more spiteful and hateful, even if you don't realize it. It's like road rage - the tiniest inconvenience leads to speeding, honking, tailgating, cursing, flipping someone the finger - if you asked that same person why they did that an hour later, they wouldn't have an honest answer for you.

Now take that man, and drop into his life a lucky young lady who's oblivious enough to show basic human courtesy, who's willing to listen and share in a way nobody in his life has before, and watch the succession of events unfold. We all know a story like this, and we all know how it ends. 

Movies like *Taxi Driver* or *Joker* aren't a testament to a society has failed men, they're parodies of the inner worlds these people create for themselves. That's why it's so difficult, and so lame, to talk about. Saying it's men's fault and that they need to step up and do better only antagonizes them more and cements their position further. But also, saying that society has failed men suggests that this behavior is acceptable, and expected, and men are simply powerless against their own minds.

So what is the solution? Is there a magical incel cure? Can we fix men?

## to the man in the mirror

We can start with ourselves, and the people closest to us first. Be around someone enough, and you'll start to talk like them, act like them, think like them, and the reverse is true as well.

Check in on your friends, remember their birthdays. Get together with your group and surprise someone who hasn't been doing so hot lately. Ask them how their siblings are doing. Small things to bring you together. When the time's right, get into some real shit. Timing is key of course, and there's a time and place for every kind of conversation. But just be aware, and be ready. It really is a team sport, and trying to make it through alone isn't doing you or anyone around you any favors. But before your friends, start with yourself. You have to be someone they look up to, not down at, to make a difference.

Are you truly, really honest with yourself? Does that thing really not bother you as much as you say it does? Why not?

The cure to male loneliness might just be out on the pickleball court.
