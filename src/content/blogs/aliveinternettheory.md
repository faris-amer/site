# alive internet theory
## the dead internet theory
The dead internet theory stems from [this post](https://forum.agoraroad.com/index.php?threads/dead-internet-theory-most-of-the-internet-is-fake.3011/) on an online forum called Agora Road that's a relic of the old internet, similar to many other forums/imageboards from the era (careful if you decide to visit, it's pretty edgy). Agora road is a tribute to the vaporwave aesthetic, and a forum that is "the best kept secret on the internet":

![](/media/pictures/agroraroad.png)

The post is a mix of wistful nostalgia and doomsday-cult conspiracy, but it speaks to a much broader feeling that's permeated the internet for a few years now. The post starts: 

>"My goal by posting this seemingly jumbled mess is to... how can I put it? I want you to think, I want you to be aware, to digest all this. Because on a basic level I love you all. I feel like we're all in this together, this dangerous game we did not choose to play and which I think is kicking into high gear. I do not hold many answers and don't have all the pieces of the puzzle, but I AM aware there is a puzzle. Please feel free to go wild with all of this. Post it wherever you want, on whatever site you want or use. I am a nobody like you, and what matters to me is only that this reaches you and as many people as possible. At worst you'll be entertained or kill time."
><br/>
><br/>
>"The Internet feels empty and devoid of people. It is also devoid of content. Compared to the Internet of say 2007 (and beyond) the Internet of today is entirely sterile. There is nowhere to go and nothing to do, see, read or experience anymore. It all imploded into a handful of normal--- sites and these empty husks we inhabit. Yes, the Internet may seem gigantic, but it's like a hot air balloon with nothing inside. Some of this is absolutely the fault of corporations and government entities."

The general theory is that sometime in 2016 the internet became flooded with bots to drown out the voices of real people and push whatever we're interested in - and subvert the masses for some shadowy agenda. Bots posing as humans to the point of fooling the majority may seem like a stretch, but take a look at [this](https://www.reddit.com/r/SubSimulatorGPT2/) and you may do a double take next time you scroll through a comment section.
Conspiracies aside, it's pretty clear to anyone who's spent time online in the past ~10ish years that the internet is just not the same anymore, and everything feels... worse. Is this just a result of everyone growing up, or has the internet truly gotten worse?

## the infinite content conveyor belt
there is nothing in this world that fills me with more dread and resentment than the black-box sewage pipe of content that is the modern social media algorithm. The fact that what I am being shown is being decided by some mathematical function written by some of the smartest people in the world maximizing for my engagement is absurd, and I feel nothing short of hate and humiliation for every second I spend scrolling. It's an infernal hamster wheel of slop content that never ends, never nourishes, never feels "done". By the end of your scroll session, you've seen the things your friends have liked and read so many comments and felt the energy of so many "people" course through you, so many emotions, both of your own and others', and yet.. it all feels empty. Social media is awesome, and there's a lot to be said about it's benefits and detriments - but I really think we're in the worst of it right now. 

An algorithm has no taste, cannot feel inspired, cannot laugh, has no sense of empathy, cannot feel guilt or shame, it does not know right from wrong, nor anger, nor love; an algorithm exists for the sole purpose of serving you the most amount of advertisements possible in a given period of time. And this is the worst part. 
"The medium is the message". The same ways that directors will hide blood or violence in movies to get a PG rating for more ticket sales or the way music is censored for playing on the radio, content gets shifted and twisted by the algorithm in ways that we don't ever expect. Content that takes you out of the flow state of scrolling is discouraged, so the #1 "news" app on the app store (twitter) discourages and [deprioritizes tweets with links in them](https://x.com/TheBubbleBubble/status/1849818873018610090?lang=en)! Surely serving 280 character breaking news with no references is a good strategy to spread true and objective news, right? All content is made to be "family friendly" (see: advertiser-approved) which influences *every* piece of media we see, and in a cyclic effect affects the words we use and the language around us. I've never wanted to "unalive" myself more than the first time I heard "unalived" in a spoken conversation.

Don't worry, the algorithm can't hurt us here - we can use real words to describe things.
## looking into the trough
Content optimized for an algorithm optimized for keeping your attention results in many bizarre results that somehow work - content that should never have been created or spread. Entertainment exists to entertain, but through the algorithm we've managed to crossbreed an absolute freak - something like pure, concentrated entertainment. It would never have been created in nature; nobody wants to watch this, and nobody really *wants* to make this. It's called "brainrot" for a reason. But it's easily accessible, easily digested, has no *apparent* health effects, and zero calories - we've made the Splenda of entertainment. The worst offenders of algorithm abomination fall into two categories: ragebait and slop. Slop content is content that's just uncanny enough to be believable and catch your attention, but if you took more than a second to process what's going on your mind contorts itself from the absurdity, resulting in dismissal and moving on, or a half hour of trying to find meaning in something that never had any. Ragebait can be anything from a hot take to celebrity drama to impending threats of world war 3 - often very politically/emotionally charged and extremely shallow, devoid of any nuance or anything worth looking at past a quick "hot take" or quip intended to spark controversy.

It's bizarre how as soon as anything happens, it's like there's this content machine that generates every single take you could possibly have, and then serves you the one you most agree with - and also the one on the opposite side. Then in the comments, the counter-takes and the counter-counter takes, all for you to sift through and come out reaffirmed that you've definitely had the correct point of view all along. After all, there's so many people saying the same thing, it must be true.

And that's what takes me to my main thesis: *I never asked to see any of this.*
Back in the old days, I remember youtube having two main pages: Subscriptions, and Trending. Instagram was the same, with your main feed showing you only people you follow, and the explore page being everything else. Now, it's almost like I see everything *except* the people I follow, and it drives me insane. Any time you see a reel or a post that makes you feel agitated, angry, disgusted, weirded out, just remember - you never asked to see any of this, and you have no choice in the matter. You can't just follow other accounts or unfollow that one, because the algorithm is in control, you aren't. 
I know the people in my life, and I know what creators I care about or want to see - I don't care about anything else. I don't want to see random gore videos or photos of war and strife or anything political unless I specifically ask for it. Why do my news, music, war footage, comedy, charity, friend's birthday post, memes, and another AI assistant ad present themselves in the exact same format, one after another? Isn't this a little insane?
## curation & the lo-fi hiphopification of everything
I like media, especially music, a lot. I love finding a good album or a really well-made movie or a youtube video with *soul*. Media spreads in many ways, but looking back, the way I've found all of my favorite shows, albums, and books has never been through an algorithm, it's always been through someone i know, or someone on the internet. I remember in 2016 browsing through the /mu/sic board of a certain website-that-shall-not-be-named and found people who were really passionate - sometimes to an extreme - about music. People that had cataloged entire libraries of albums I've never heard of before and sorted them by genre, release year, personal ratings - it was all very biased, but it was very human. I loved the experience of going somewhere, seeing someone's thoughts on something, listening to it, replying with my own - without the pressure of maintaining a following or a persona or getting buried in the thread. It sounds pretty cliche to say it out loud but it really did make music much more than just something to throw on in the background. 

Music subcultures and music curation have been a thing since the dawn of time - in the before times, music journalism was the way people found new music. Different magazines existed for different people, and you read whatever you were looking for: Rolling Stone for the mainstream, XXL for hip-hop and rap, mixmag for electronic, and if you're a real nerd - The Wire (who's 500th issue releases as I'm writing this). I was too late for this era of music curation - but just in time to catch the tail-end of the internet music culture that existed on forums like rateyourmusic. While those sites are still semi-active, a lot of it has died off, and the culture has become very toxic - everyone grew old and grumpy and gatekeep-y - there's a lot of stereotypes about these communities now from all over the internet. People loved the music sharing and the community aspect - but hated the backdrop of edgy, mostly unmoderated internet, and slowly began to leave the site. I'll never forget finding Sweet Trip or Vince Staples for the first time because someone posted a .png of a funny looking fish anonymously for nothing more than a reply going "good taste".

![](/media/pictures/musicbros.png)

The thing that led me down this memory to begin with is a new spotify feature, daylists.
your daylist is a playlist that rotates throughout the day and has whimsical titles like "hipster indie wednesday night" or "dreamy hazy thursday morning" or "paranormal dark cabaret saturday afternoon" and what they promise is a vibe. Vibe-based listening is very easy, and comes very naturally. Listening to music is almost never the main activity, there's always something going on in the background. You're driving or cleaning or studying - and there lies the start of this all. The first instance of infinite, vibe-based music that I can remember is lofi hip hop beats to study/relax to, the youtube livestream. Lofi music is inoffensive, easy to consume, helps some people focus, sets a warm and cozy mood - you could have it on for hours and not be able to name a single song, but still enjoy it. Not to say that lofi is bad or cheap music, but I hate the idea that *everything* has to be this way. I want to listen to, look at, read, watch things that **I** chose to, and when I want recommendations, I'll go look for those. 

*"But you can just ignore that playlist"* I hear you say, sure, but my gripe isn't just with the playlist. It's with the way the whole culture is moving, towards this future where everything is infinite and blends together, everything is just unsatisfying or unstimulating enough to keep you going - you can't just listen to an album, you need inserted smart recommendations and everything shuffled and the album radio after it's over so you're always streaming something and making the number go up. This also leads to another medium-is-the-message moment, where artists start making boring music that doesn't even register enough in your head to consider skipping, or music that's easily reccommendable and playlist-able (who the hell is sombr), because almost nobody's going out of their way to search up artists or albums looking for new music, you're only exposed to algorithm reccomendations. Even when searching something like "gym playlist" to find someone else's playlist, you find a list of ill-fitting spotify-generated playlists "made for you" with the same songs you've listened to before. Spotify comes off as a tasteless, tone-deaf stalker that keeps bringing you bunches of pre-blown dandelions because you said you liked flowers once. *"I never asked you to make me this!"* I say, as I throw the proverbial bouquet of bot-generated playlists to the floor, and storm off into the distance.

![](/media/pictures/young-caucasian-boy-disheartened-throwing-away-flowers.jpg)

Netflix has also seen the effects of this "lo-fi hiphopification" where a new term, "second-screen viewing" has made the rounds. There's a reason why Netflix shows and movies have a reputation for being *like that*, it's because screenwriters are being told to make worse shows and movies on purpose. There's a reason you can't watch something like Better Call Saul in the background while you scroll or text someone - the show is intense, and a lot of exposition happens outside of characters speaking to each other. It gives the cinema effect, where you don't want to look away or get up to pee because you're afraid you'll miss the next important scene - and if you are distracted while watching, when you regain focus you'll be confused and unsure of what's going on, ruining your experience. [This article](https://www.nplusonemag.com/issue-49/essays/casual-viewing/) on casual viewing goes in-depth on this topic, talking about how Netflix keeps breaking viewing records for its original shows and movies. An excerpt from the article:

>Such slipshod filmmaking works for the streaming model, since audiences at home are often barely paying attention. Several screenwriters who’ve worked for the streamer told me a common note from company executives is “have this character announce what they’re doing so that viewers who have this program on in the background can follow along.” 

Because the only metric we use to value media is views and watch time, things that are easy to watch, forgettable, and appeal to a wide audience rise to the top of your feeds. Because of this, studios that create this "tide-pod cinema" get more funding and more seasons and movies or TV shows that may be "better" in their message, their characters, their impact on people after the fact are left in the dust. There's a place for everything, and I'm not saying that these shows are bad or that you're a bad person if you enjoy them, I'm just constantly put off by the forced promotion of shows made to be forgotten. 

## sippin on haterade

Again, a common theme arises: I never chose to see this, and it's being shown to me by an algorithm with unknown motivations. And people know that they don't like it; almost everyone who uses tiktok talks about it like they're doing some illicit substance: "I limit myself to 1 hour per day", "I uninstalled the app and only use it in my browser so I'm less tempted", "I only look at the stuff my friends send me anyways". But really, it is enjoyable. In a world where everyone's so busy and it feels like you'll never have any time to yourself, being able to take short 10-15 minute scroll breaks to kill the silence in an awkward car ride, on the bus, waiting in line for a coffee, it's pretty awesome to have engineered, distilled, pure entertainment at your fingertips. You never have to wait for the next issue, the next episode, the next video, it's all available at an instant. Which is pretty sweet! My issue is with the algorithm, and the way it divides us over and over. The algorithm is a bit insane, and seems to have no regard for the things I actually like. 

This whole "I never asked to see this" leads to a TON of hate and strife online. Take for example, some 10th grader who's just started an art page to show off their drawings for a few of their friends. They make silly little videos and a few strangers start following alongside their friends, encouraging them to keep improving, etc - the internet has some cool people. Soon enough though, and one of their videos skyrockets in popularity. Their art isn't very good, but it's not like they're drawing to prove a point or trying to make profit or a business out of it, so who cares? 

Unfortunately, millions of people who never asked to see some random person's amateur art that's maybe jarringly ugly or of an unorthodox style or of a subject they don't like are now being presented with this. of a million, if even 1% left negative comments (and let's be real it's a lot more than 1%) that's thousands of hate comments to someone who's maybe ever kept a few dozen friends at any point in time. And then if 1% of people are negative, my (somehow still enduring) faith in humanity would have me believe that more people would write positive comments, or at least be driven to say mean things to the mean people (because that makes everything better, right?). But the mean people are *really* driven and so a few hours later and this poor soul is dealing with countless personal attacks and drama and negativity and online haters - all because they wanted to share something they loved with their friends. "If you post something on the internet then prepare to have everyone see it" is a common warning, but is it really their fault? I see it happen so often, and while it does lead to some careers taking off or people getting the support they need to pursue their passion full time - I honestly couldn't care less 😭.

> but you're 3000 words into this ran-

Listen, they're free to do whatever they want, but I don't care!! That's my whole point. I don't want to see this. I never asked to see this. I don't care about the art, I don't care about the hate comments, I don't care about the roasts and counter-roasts in the comments, I don't want to see this 185 comment chain that ends in statements I feel gross reading aloud. But you know what? I see a shitty drawing, I click on the comments, and I read them all. Because as much as I present as enlightened and above silly Instagram reels I am in fact not immune to the pull of a terrible homemade video with over ten thousand comments. But I hate it! It leaves me feeling more negative, and on days or weeks where I'm doomscrolling a lot, I find that I'm more irritable and generally just slightly more tired and less energetic. it feels like I'm a donkey with a carrot hanging over my head - maybe the next video will be good, I swear....

## still living

so what's the solution? well, it's self-evident. If the problem is that I keep seeing things I don't care about, I have to start finding things to care about! Curating your own 'feed' is difficult, because often the things worth seeing are paywalled, or have bad advertising. It's your job to take back control of your attention span - be your own algotrithm! there's sooo much out there to read, watch, hear. And it doesn't all have to be artsy or cerebral or educational, you can consume "bad" media, as long as you're making the conscious decision to. making these small decisions every day, what you want to spend your time doing. There's no worse feeling than getting home and doomscrolling as the hours pass you by, and at least for me, there's no better feeling than finishing a book or movie or youtube video or lecture series and sitting there after it's done. Some time with your own thoughts is important, what did YOU think of it? What moments mattered to you? Sure, it's well made and highly rated etc etc but did it impact you in any way? these kinda of thoughts are lost on us, but it's where the inspiration for new, real things comes into place. Even if you're not interested in making things per se, it makes you a much more interesting person.

It's important not to gatekeep, and to share the things we think, and the inspirations we draw from. Subscribed to a really cool newsletter or watched a video that gave you a new perspective on things? Instead of parroting the lines word-for-word to your friends and impressing them with how much you know about the topic, share your sources, and build on top of it. You read this interesting story, what do you think of it? You listened to this cool podcast on your commute, share it! and expand on it.
